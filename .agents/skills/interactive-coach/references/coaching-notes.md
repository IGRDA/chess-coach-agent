# Coaching notes

## Pitching by level

### Beginner

- Name one idea at a time and use ordinary chess language.
- Prefer a short forcing line to a numerical evaluation.
- Connect the move to a visible feature: an undefended piece, exposed king, passed
  pawn, or simple mating pattern.
- Ask one focused question rather than several.

### Intermediate

- Compare candidate moves and name the opponent's best defensive resource.
- Tie tactics to positional causes such as weak squares, poor coordination, or an
  unsafe king.
- Turn the answer into a reusable calculation or review habit.

### Advanced

- Discuss move-order details, tradeoffs, and the strongest rejected alternative.
- Use principal variations selectively; do not dump raw engine lines.
- Distinguish a stable strategic edge from a temporary tactical evaluation.

## Motifs worth naming

Name a motif only when the evidence supports it: pin, skewer, fork, discovered
attack, deflection, decoy, clearance, interference, removing the defender,
overloading, back-rank mate, mating net, zwischenzug, trapped piece, or promotion
tactic.

For endgames, prefer transferable methods and explain the operative rule:

- **Opposition and key squares:** the kings' geometry determines whether a pawn can
  advance; occupy the key square with the king before pushing when the pawn needs
  support.
- **Square of the pawn:** imagine a square from the pawn to its promotion rank. If the
  defending king cannot enter that square on its move, the pawn can run without king
  help. Count tempos before making a slower king move.
- **Lucena:** build a rook bridge to shelter the king from checks and promote.
- **Philidor:** hold the third rank, then check from behind after the pawn advances.
- **Wrong bishop and rook pawn:** the ending draws when the bishop cannot control the
  promotion corner and the defender's king reaches it.
- **Réti idea:** use a diagonal king route to chase one pawn while supporting another.
- **Queen-and-king mate:** use the queen to shrink a box, bring the king to support
  it, and check only when the box gets smaller. Before the final net, always check for
  stalemate; random queen checks teach neither coordination nor stalemate control.

## Reading move verdicts

An engine verdict describes how much evaluation a move concedes; it does not replace
the coaching diagnosis. Praise a genuinely strong move, but when a non-best move
wastes a tempo, misses a forcing resource, or uses unreliable technique, explain that
specific habit even if the objective win or draw remains. Show what the best move
exploits now and what the student's move would allow against stronger resistance.

## Spoiler control

A hint or method request should narrow the search without encoding the answer. Point
to the tactical feature or candidate category first; never copy a move from supplied
engine facts merely because it is visible. Once the student explicitly asks for the
move, state the current position's engine move unambiguously and explain the evidence
behind it.
